Egypt Closed the Consumer Finance Door. The Licence Is Now the Asset.

Three FRA decisions in sixteen months have turned an Egyptian consumer finance licence from a compliance cost into a scarce asset. Most diligence still prices the credit book.

On 24 February 2026, Egypt's Financial Regulatory Authority suspended acceptance of new applications to incorporate and license consumer finance companies, for one year from 25 February. The decision matters less for what it blocks than for what it removes: the last cheap way in.

Three Decisions, One Direction

The sequence is the story. In October 2024, FRA Resolution 184 paused new licences for consumer finance and microfinance companies, but fintech firms operating under Law No. 5 of 2022 were exempt. In October 2025, Resolution 237 extended the pause by another year, and the FRA said it needed the time to assess the financial solvency of existing companies. Four months later, the February 2026 decision closed the fintech exemption too.

Each step tightened the previous one. None loosened it. A pause that is extended and then widened is not a pause. It is a policy, and the stated rationale, solvency and AML oversight of the incumbents, points at the existing players, not at the applicants.

Why the Fintech Route Mattered

The fintech route was the door a venture-backed entrant could actually walk through. FRA Decision 268 of 2023 set minimum capital at EGP 15 million per licensed activity, with technology specialists holding at least 25% of the capital. That is a startup-sized cheque, set against a market that is not startup-sized. With that route shut, a new lender cannot be created. It can only be bought, or rented through a partnership.

The Market the Pause Protects

The incumbents are growing fast. Reported full-year 2025 consumer finance volume was about EGP 96.3 billion, up 57.1% year on year, per press coverage of FRA data. valU, the largest BNPL player, reports roughly 23% market share and a 0.92% non-performing loan ratio at 3Q25 in its own investor presentation. Those two numbers together are the point: rapid growth, clean reported credit quality, and a regulator that has stopped adding competitors.

When a regulator freezes entry in a market growing above 50%, the licence stops being a cost of doing business and starts behaving like a scarce asset. Diligence that models the loan book and ignores the licence is pricing the wrong thing.

The carve-outs show how the scarcity is already being allocated. The FRA approved Souhoola, Banque Misr's consumer finance subsidiary, to operate on fintech infrastructure, and Malaz received approval to enter consumer finance, both on applications filed before the cut-off. Access now goes to the bank-backed and the early.

What the Investment Memo Usually Misses

On the Africa IC Checklist, this sits mainly in regulatory risk and exit pathway, and it cuts both ways. For the holder of a licence, the moat is regulatory, not product-based, which is a weaker moat than it looks. The same authority that built it is explicitly assessing solvency, and the 2024 pause was framed around Basel III-style capital checks. A regulator that defends incumbents by closing the door can also tighten what incumbents must hold.

For the investor outside the licence perimeter, the pause converts a build decision into a buy decision. That raises the price of licensed shells and makes acquirer-led exits more plausible, but it is a thesis, not an observed fact. I have not found a disclosed transaction price for a licensed Egyptian consumer lender, and no figure here should be read as a valuation benchmark.

I traced a similar dynamic in WAEMU, where the regulator's leverage over a dominant operator turned out to be weaker than the rulebook implied. Egypt is the opposite case: here the regulator is the scarce-asset allocator, and the operator has to work within it. The wider pattern of rules being written around what incumbents can absorb also appears in the stablecoin infrastructure question.

The Date That Matters

The current suspension runs to roughly late February 2027. The previous pause was extended at its expiry. If this one is extended too, the realistic planning assumption for any entrant is that the licensing route stays shut for the life of a typical fund's investment period, not for another twelve months.

The IC question: in any Egyptian consumer credit deal on your desk, are you underwriting the loan book or the licence, and what happens to the valuation if the FRA's solvency review changes what the licence requires you to hold?
