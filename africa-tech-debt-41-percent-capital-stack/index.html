41% of African Tech Capital Is Now Debt. Your Equity Sits Behind It.

Debt was 17% of African tech funding in 2019 and 41% in 2025. Read as a distress signal, the number is wrong. Read as a continental trend, it is wrong again. It is a receivables story, it is concentrated in one market, and it changes what an equity investment committee is actually underwriting.

Partech's 2025 Africa Tech Venture Capital report put total African tech funding at $4.1B. Debt was $1.64B of it, up 63% year on year. Equity grew 8%, to roughly $2.4B. Debt's share of total capital deployed moved from 31% in 2024 to 41% in 2025, and from 17% in 2019.

Most coverage filed that under "founders can't raise equity, so they borrow." The equity line says otherwise. Equity did not shrink. Debt simply grew eight times faster in dollar terms. That is not a market running out of risk capital. It is a market where lenders found something they are willing to lend against.

Lenders Are Not Underwriting Startups. They Are Underwriting Collections.

Look at what absorbed the largest cheques. d.light expanded a receivables financing facility by a reported $300M in July 2025. Sun King closed a reported $156M local-currency securitisation in Kenya the same month, described at the time as the largest of its kind in sub-Saharan Africa outside South Africa. Both are pay-as-you-go businesses. Both convert a device sold on credit into a stream of small mobile-money repayments.

A lender looking at those businesses is not asking whether the product will find a market. That question was answered years ago. The lender is asking a narrower one: of every 100 shillings owed this month, how many arrive, through which rail, and how quickly does the device lock if they do not.

The collateral is not the solar panel or the phone. It is the collection engine: agent coverage, mobile money penetration, and remote lock-out. Debt flows to whoever has already built distribution that collects.

That is why the 41% clusters where it does. Debt capacity in African tech is a function of the distribution stack underneath the company, not the company's stage or sector label. A Series A business with a mature, data-rich receivables book can borrow. A Series C marketplace with no receivables cannot, however strong its growth.

The 41% Is a Kenya Number Wearing a Continental Label

Partech's country split breaks the headline apart. Kenya raised $498M in debt, about 30% of all African tech debt, and debt was 48% of everything deployed into Kenyan tech in 2025. South Africa raised $72M in debt, about 10% of its total, with debt volumes down 45% year on year. Egypt's debt reached $246M, up 73%. Nigeria's reached $160M, up 132%, from a small base.

Kenya is where mobile money, agent density and PAYGo operating history overlap most completely. South Africa has deep local credit markets, so tech companies there have less reason to route debt through the venture channel at all. Nigeria's growth rate is real, but $160M across Africa's largest startup market says lenders are still pricing in something Kenya does not carry.

That something is currency. A dollar loan against naira receivables is a bet on the exchange rate as much as on the borrower. The Sun King transaction matters precisely because it was raised in Kenyan shillings: the asset and the liability sit in the same currency. Where that matching is not available, the debt either does not get written or it gets written at a price that consumes the margin it was meant to fund.

What Changes for the Equity Holder

When debt is 41% of the capital in the room, equity is no longer the whole capital structure. It is the residual claim. Three things follow, and none of them show up in a standard pitch deck.

First, covenants govern the business before the board does. Receivables facilities typically carry portfolio-quality tests. If repayment rates slip below the threshold, the borrowing base shrinks, and growth stops being a board decision. An equity investor who has not read the facility agreement does not know the real operating constraint.

Second, the exit is shaped by the senior claim. AVCA recorded 81 African private capital exits in 2025, and trade sales remain the dominant route. A trade buyer acquiring a debt-heavy asset-finance business is buying a loan book and the collection machine behind it. The equity cheque at exit is what remains after the lenders, and that number moves with portfolio quality, not with revenue growth.

Third, the distribution layer is now a credit variable. Agent churn, mobile money downtime or a change in wallet interoperability rules used to be growth risks. In a receivables-funded company they are collection risks, which means they are covenant risks. I made a related argument in the World Bank tenders piece: in African markets, whoever controls the last mile controls the economics. The 41% puts a price on that control.

Where the Pattern Stops

This is not an argument that debt is good or that equity is overvalued. It is an argument that the two are now underwriting different things. Debt is underwriting proven collections in currency-matched markets. Equity is underwriting everything that has not yet produced a collectable receivable.

For businesses with no receivables, the 41% is irrelevant except as a signal of where institutional capital is comfortable. For businesses that do carry receivables, it is the most important number in the data room, and it is usually in a facility agreement rather than the model. The Africa IC Checklist already separates distribution economics from currency risk. In a debt-funded company, those two dimensions collapse into one question.

The IC Question

Before the next equity cheque into an African asset-finance or embedded-credit business, ask the company for one number and one document: the trailing twelve-month collection rate by cohort and rail, and the covenant schedule of every senior facility. If the covenant headroom is thinner than the collection rate's worst historical month, the equity is not buying growth. It is buying an option on the lender's patience.
