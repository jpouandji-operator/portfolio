Egypt Closed the Consumer Finance Door. The Licence Is the Asset Now.

Egyptian regulators froze new consumer finance licences while the sector grew 57% in a year. The investable question is no longer who has the best app. It is who already holds a licence and a funding line.

Consumer finance in Egypt reached EGP 96.3 billion by the end of 2025, up 57% year on year, across more than 10.8 million customers and 48 licensed companies [UNVERIFIED — PENDING SOURCE: FRA/EnterpriseAM figure]. In the same stretch, regulators stopped issuing new licences.

The Freeze

On 24 February 2026, the Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA) issued Decree No. 43, suspending new applications to incorporate and license consumer finance companies under the FinTech Law for one year, effective 25 February [UNVERIFIED — PENDING SOURCE: FRA decree text]. Law-firm guides describe it as extending the earlier Decree No. 184 of 2024, and as covering traditional and fintech-platform channels alike. A separate report says the central bank extended a suspension on new consumer finance and microfinance licences for a further year [UNVERIFIED: the two reports attribute the freeze to different regulators].

Read that against the volume. A regulator does not freeze entry into a market it considers small or stable. It freezes entry into a market it considers to be growing faster than its supervision.

When a regulator closes entry, the licence stops being a compliance cost and becomes the asset. Forty-eight holders now own something nobody can apply for.

The Door Is Not Fully Shut

The freeze is narrower than "Egypt closed fintech." In May 2026 the FRA approved six new fintech and digital-insurance entities [UNVERIFIED — PENDING SOURCE: Financial Afrik, 11 May 2026], and the draft fintech law approved by its board earlier in the year is still being shaped. Payments, wallets and insurance are being licensed. Consumer credit is the category being held back.

That split is the signal. Egypt is happy to onboard rails. It is rationing the activity where the customer ends up owing money.

The Funding Line Is the Second Gate

One report indicates the central bank has tightened how banks deal with non-bank financing companies, including prior approval for certain securitised-bond purchases [UNVERIFIED — PENDING SOURCE: CBE circular text and date]. If confirmed, the effect is a second gate. Non-bank lenders in Egypt fund their books substantially through bank credit lines and securitisation. Control that channel and you control growth, whatever licence a company holds.

This changes what an investor should diligence. A licence without a committed funding line is a permit to stand still. In this market, the question for any consumer-credit target is how much of next year's book is already funded, and by whom.

The Affordability Flag

The regulatory concern is visible in the product. One cited expert points to apps offering credit lines up to EGP 250,000 to users earning EGP 6,000 to 8,000 a month [UNVERIFIED — PENDING SOURCE]. A line worth roughly 30 to 40 months of income is not an inclusion product. It is a credit-quality problem that arrives on a delay, and regulators who watched 57% growth have read the same arithmetic.

I traced the same pattern in the BCEAO's handling of Wave: the regulator's real constraint is rarely the text of the rule. It is how much leverage it holds over the fastest-growing player. Egypt is using licensing and funding instead of deadlines, and Egypt's pound history, a roughly 50% fall against the dollar between 2022 and 2023 [see GON Benchmark FX-003; CBE/IMF source to be re-verified], explains why it is cautious about unsecured household leverage.

What This Does to a Portfolio

Three consequences follow, none of them in the headline.

Entry by licence acquisition replaces entry by application. With applications frozen, the only route into Egyptian consumer credit is buying or partnering with a holder. Prices for those holders should move before the freeze lifts. The same logic drove the infrastructure premium in the IHS towers deal: when the asset cannot be rebuilt, the owner sets the terms.

Pan-African fintechs with an Egypt expansion slide have a timing problem. A "launch lending in Egypt in 2027" plan assumes an application route that currently does not exist for the freeze period.

Incumbent growth is capped by funding, not demand. Demand is clearly there. A book that grows 57% only while banks keep lending to it is a book with a dependency the cap table does not show.

Operators who have run credit products across African markets know that the regulator's calendar is a product dependency. Nothing in a deck captures it, which is exactly why it is mispriced.

The IC Question

If your Egyptian consumer-credit target holds a licence today, ask for the maturity schedule of its bank funding lines and whether any renewal needs the regulator's sign-off. If it does not hold one, ask what you are actually underwriting: a licence you can buy, or a plan that assumes a door that is closed.
